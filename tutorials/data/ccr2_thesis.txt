31. Public Summary
We developed a generative workflow using DrugEx and ROCS to speed up drug discovery.
DrugEx, a generative AI model, creates new molecules, while ROCS gives a score based on
3D shapes. Focusing on CCR2, a protein involved in cancer, our method identified interesting
drug candidates for further development.
Keywords
DrugEx, ROCS, CCR2 inhibitors, reinforcement learning, pharmacophore modelling
2. Abstract
The vast chemical space of approximately 1060 molecules presents a significant challenge for
novel drug discovery, necessitating advanced techniques to streamline the process. Currently,
43 percent of research and development expenditure is allocated to the preclinical phase.
Despite advancements in data collection on ligand properties and therapeutic targets, enhanced
computational methods are essential in the preclinical phase for leveraging available data to
identify novel drug candidates. We present an integrated computational workflow combining
DrugEx, a de novo molecular generator utilizing reinforcement learning (RL), with ROCS, a
3D ligand-based shape-matching tool, to accelerate the identification of intracellular allosteric
antagonists targeting the chemokine receptor CCR2, a G-protein coupled receptor involved in
inflammatory diseases and cancer metastasis. Using a pharmacophore model built with ROCS
and validated against a CCR2 allosteric ligand dataset, DrugEx was trained to generate
structures likely to bind the intracellular allosteric site of CCR2. Subsequently, a large set of
generated structures was scored with ROCS and evaluated via docking with AutoDock Vina.
The docking studies identified promising antagonists.
This workflow demonstrated the scientific relevance of a de novo approach by enabling the
efficient generation and screening of novel drug candidates. By integrating RL and 3D ligand-
based screening, our approach enhances the exploration of chemical space and optimizes
molecular properties. This methodology holds promise for broader applications in drug
discovery, highlighting the need for ongoing data refinement of known drug-like compounds
as references to improve the virtual screening and generation of novel drug candidates.
Furthermore, novel promising antagonists were identified that may inhibit inflammatory
diseases through upregulation of CCR2.
43. Graphical abstract
4. Introduction
The potential number of novel drug candidates is estimated at 1060 1. Due to the sheer size of
potential drug candidates, modern techniques are required for faster and less resource-intensive
drug discovery. Currently, 43 percent of research and development expenditure is allocated to
the preclinical phase2. Despite significant advancements in data collection regarding ligand
properties and therapeutic targets, enhanced computational methods are essential to fully
leverage the available data3 and discover novel drug-candidates.
De novo drug design (DNDD) is mainly used for the exploration of novel molecules. The
determination of the specificity of these molecules for designated targets is done through multi
objective optimisation (MOO). Reinforcement learning (RL) implements MOO objective into
the generative process through a feedback loop. The feedback loop consists of scorers that score
based on different objectives. These scores are incorporated as feedback and used as selection
tool for the generator to improve the molecules.
Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are commonly used as generator for small molecules4,5. The
data-driven training of these networks enables a selective approach to generation of novel
molecules tied to a limited chemical space, and not bound to set parameters5. RNNs with Long
Short-Term Memory (LSTM)6 units results in SMILES-based character prediction and new
SMILES-strings sampling based on those predictions7,8. The use of LSTM units has been shown
to be among the best sequence-based generators (networks with incorporated RL)9. The
validation of generated SMILES-strings is executed through the reinforcement loop.
Virtual screening protocols are used in the drug discovery process to analyse and filter
synthesized and virtual libraries. Common protocols that are designed for molecule scoring are
divided into two major categories, structure-based virtual screening (SBVS) and ligand-based
virtual screening (LBVS)10,11. Structure-based approaches employ docking to analyse three-
dimensional (3D) interactions between molecule and complex 11,12.
5Docking enables the correct positioning of a pose of a ligand within the binding site of a
receptor, identifying ligand-receptor interactions and calculating binding affinity11,12. Docking
is most effective when ligand-receptor crystal structures are available for in vitro confirmation
of receptor-ligand conformations12.
LBVS on the other hand, compares stereo- and physiochemical properties of molecules based
on the molecular similarity principle11,13. LBVS can be used in two-dimensional (2D) and three
dimensional (3D) approaches. 2D shape similarity approaches such as quantitative structure-
activity relationship (QSAR) models enable based on known active and inactive molecules the
prediction of activity values of novel molecules13,14. These QSAR models are limited by the
quality and amount of reference data available (the same limitation applies to 3D QSAR
models)14. 3D-shape similarity approaches rely on the principle that similar shapes of molecules
will results in a similar mode of binding in the binding pocket10,13.
This process of shape-similarity is commonly referred to as shape-matching and can be based
on volume, surface, alignment and atomic-distance10. One of the shape-matching methods
called pharmacophore modelling. Pharmacophore modelling requires known 3D poses of active
ligands as reference, a similar issue previously ascribed to docking. However, the number of
required reference ligands only depends on the diversity that is desired from the screened
molecules. Pharmacophore modelling is a form of shape-matching that enables the use of
limited number of reference molecules. The method incorporates the electrochemical properties
of ligands within the screening to represent docking abilities while attaining rapid analysis11,12.
Co-crystallised ligands or ligands in clinical trial have been proven as solid references10,13.
Papadopoulos et al.15 illustrated the effectiveness of combining 3D shape and pharmacophore
modelling for similarity scoring in de novo molecule design using generative models trained
with reinforcement learning. This method yielded a higher degree of chemical diversity
compared to their conventional QSAR-based workflow.
Building on this foundation, chemokine receptors, which are a class of G-protein coupled
membrane receptors activated by small, secreted proteins called chemokines, represent a
promising target for computational workflows. CCRs are involved in inflammatory and cell-
regulated pathways16. For example, expression of chemokine receptor CCR7 in cancer leads to
lymph node metastasis17. Overexpression of CCR2 is prevalent in cell division and tumour
growth, atherosclerosis and diabetes 18,19. The inhibition of specific CCRs is rather difficult due
to the non-selectivity of chemokines. Chemokines CCL2, CCL5 and CCL7 bind to CCR2, but
CCL7 has also affinity for CCR5 18,20. While inhibition via the orthosteric binding pocket may
seem as a potential drug target, non-competitive binding is prevalent. Intracellular allosteric
binding has been shown as a potential target for non-competitive inhibition21,22. The
development of potential drug candidates however, have not yet yielded any therapies. The
accurate mode of binding to the pocket remains unknown, as only one compound has been co-
crystallised22. Therefore, CCR2 inhibition remains an interesting target for drug discovery.
In this project, we present a workflow where 3D shape matching is integrated in DNDD as
scoring mechanism. To test and optimize the in silico design, we try to identify novel allosteric
modulators that bind chemokine receptor CCR2. For DNDD, DrugEx, a tool for the design of
small molecules with deep learning generative models in a multi-objective RL framework, was
employed8,23. As scorer within the RL framework, a 3D ligand-based shape matching tool
ROCS was used. ROCS structures a pharmacophore model based on the 3D-poses of reference
molecules10,13. The reference molecules consisted of the single known crystallised compound
and a variety of drug-candidates that have shown inhibitory properties for CCR2. Their 3D-
poses were determined with docking.
65. Material and Methods
5.1 Datasets
A dataset of intracellular allosteric ligands tested on CCR2 receptors (N=158) was extracted
from ChEMBL3124 and Papyrus25 based on SMARTS patterns on known substructures (Figure
1) and filtered on allosteric properties. Additional allosteric ligands from Peace et al.26 were
extracted from ChEMBL and added to the dataset and named CCR2ALL (N=191). This dataset
was split in a train (CCR2TRAIN) and test (CCR2TEST) set with a random split of 20 percent.
CCR2TEST was separated on actives (CCR2ACTIVE) and inactives based on a pChEMBL
value of seven or higher. The inactives lacked structural diversity, therefore the
CCR2INACTIVE dataset was constructed by calculating the descriptors of CCR2ACTIVE
and extracting molecules from CHEMBL31 based on similar parameters. Molecules with
molecular weight (MW) <= 650, LogP <= 6, hydrogen donors (NumHDonors) <=2 and
hydrogen acceptors (NumHAcceptors) <= 6 were stored in CCR2INACTIVE (N=500).

5.2 Reference molecules
To construct a ligand-based model, we utilised reference ligands, including CCR2-RA-[R], the
only co-crystallised intracellular allosteric binder22,27,28 that accurately represents allosteric
binding. Additionally, four ligands that have been in clinical-trial served as high-quality
references. Ilacirnon29 (CCX140) from Chemocentryx, initially developed for focal segmental
glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), was discontinued30,31. JNJ-2714149132,33 , Compound3934,35 and
SD-2436,37 demonstrated high affinity in binding assays. The binding conformations of these
ligands were generated with AutoDockVina (v.1.2.3)38.
75.3 Pharmacophore model
The pharmacophore model was constructed with vROCS (v. 3.6.0.1), licensed by OpenEye
Scientific Software. VROCS, a graphical user interface (GUI) tool from OpenEye, was
employed to build queries39. The parameters were set to a maximum of 5 molecules per model
and 5 models to keep. The top five models, selected based on Tanimoto similarity, were retained
for validation39, CCR2ACTIVE and CCR2INACTIVE were designated as actives and
decoys. ROCS requires 3D conformations as input. The conformers for CCR2ACTIVE and
CCR2INACTIVE were generated with OMEGA (v.5.0.0.3) in the command terminal
OpenEye-applications (v.2023.1.1)40. OMEGA and ROCS are provided within OpenEye
Toolkits (v.2023.2)41. The isomers were used as annotated in the datasets. Oeomega classic was
employed with the maximum number of conformers generated per SMILES set to 200. Model
performance of the five models was assessed by the AUC values from the Receiver Operating
Characteristics (ROC) curve42, computed by ROCS. The model exhibiting the steepest ROC
curve and highest AUC value was chosen as the final pharmacophore model.
5.4 DrugEx and synthesis scorer
For the de novo design, we employed the deep-learning generator DrugEx. The scoring module
within the RL loop included isomer generation with RDKit (for details on packages used, see
Appendix 1), conformer generation with OMEGA, and similarity scoring with ROCS. The
isomer generation was set to a maximum of 4 isomers per SMILES, with rotatable bonds and
heavy atoms limited to 15 and 45, respectively. Isomers that did not meet the criteria, were
excluded from conformer generation and screening. The criteria were based on the upper limits
of descriptors of a dataset sourced from ChEMBL31 and Papyrus specifically focused on
allosteric CCR ligands (CCRALL) and was used in generative model training. The descriptors
were calculated with RDKit. Evaluation of the synthesizability of these generated molecules
incorporated the calculation of synthesis accessibility (SA) scores43, within the DrugEx
environment. The pharmacophore model was incorporated into the reinforcement learning (RL)
loop as a similarity scorer. The ROCS score of the generated structures was determined using
the Tanimoto similarity coefficient, which is calculated based on the overlap volume between
the generated structures and the pharmacophore model44,45. The overlap volume between
molecules is determined by representing each molecule as a set of spheres. Calculating the
volume of these spheres using integral functions is complex, especially when dealing with
molecules composed of numerous spheres. Gaussian functions provide a more precise method
for calculating molecular volumes. By utilizing Gaussian contractions, these functions allow
for rapid and accurate volume calculations when comparing the volumes of two structures.
Consequently, the molecular shape of the pharmacophore model is represented by Gaussian
contractions, facilitating efficient computation of the Tanimoto coefficient.45,46. The script is
open-source47.
The generative models of DrugEx were trained using transfer learning8 , a technique involving
the refinement of a pretrained model on CCRALL. The parameters for the fine-tuned model of
each generative model were configured to run for 1000 epochs, where an epoch represents one
complete iteration over the entire dataset to update the model. The validation dataset comprised
8either 1000 or 10000 samples. To manage the computational load of large datasets, the batch
size—the number of samples processed together—was set to 128 or 256. A patience parameter
of thirty or 1000 epochs was implemented, meaning that if no improvement in the model was
observed within thirty consecutive epochs, the training process was discontinued.8. An
additional parameter, epsilon was optimised for different values. The epsilon is a parameter that
varied between 0 and 1, influencing the similarity of generated molecules to the CCRALL
dataset. Values explored for epsilon included 0.01, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4. The generative models
were evaluated based on the desired ratio, unique ratio, and ROCS score per epoch. A
compound was classified as desired if its modifier scores surpassed the thresholds of each
objective8. A compound was considered unique if its corresponding SMILES differed from the
complete list of generated molecules15.
5.5 Scoring of the generated structures
The scored structures were clustered based on a distance threshold, representing the maximum
allowable distance between structures to be grouped together48. The most divergent merges in
the clustering of the generated structures occurred at a distance above 0.7, as shown in Figure
2. Consequently, this value was set as the distance threshold to maximize the number of unique
clusters with similar structures. After, the top three highest ROCS scores per cluster were
docked into the crystallised representation allosteric binding pocket of CCR2 (PDB code
5T1A22) to evaluate their binding conformations. Docking was performed using AutoDock
Vina (v.1.2.3)38. AutoDock Vina estimates the strength of non-covalent interactions by
calculating interaction scores as binding affinities, which represent the change in free energy
upon ligand binding to a receptor49,50. The 3D visualization of the complexes were visualized
in the analysis part of the workflow as shown in Figure 3, with protein-ligand interaction
profiler (PLIP)51 and the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (Pymol), Schrödinger, LLC52.
The complete list of used packages is displayed in Appendix 1.

6.1 most optimised pharmacophore model
Pharmacophore models were constructed using vROCS based on reference molecules including
CCR2-RA-[R], JNJ-27141491 (JNJ), SD-24, CCX140, and compound 39 (Comp39). The
binding conformations of the reference molecules were determined with docking. The optimal
model was selected based on calculated AUC values and visual inspection of ROC curves.
Figure 4 illustrates these models alongside their respective reference molecules. Figure 5
presents ROC curves for Model_1 (CCR2-RA-[R], CCX140, SD-24, Comp39, JNJ) and
Model_3 (CCR2-RA-[R], CCX140, SD-24, Comp39), showing highest early enrichment.
Model_2 (CCR2-RA-[R], CCX140, SD-24, JNJ) and Model_4 (CCR2-RA-[R], CCX140, JNJ)
exhibit lower early enrichment but higher later enrichment compared to Model_1 and Model_3.
Table 2 demonstrates that Model_2 (AUC 0.941), Model_3 (0.965), Model_4 (0.956) did not
differ significantly from Model_1 (0.955). Model_5 (0.8) had the lowest performance. The
observed differences in ROC curve between Model_1 and Model_3 versus Model_2 and
Model_4 can be attributed to the composition of the validation dataset, which notably includes
a substantial number of molecules featuring a imidazole pyridine scaffold, similar to Comp39.

difference in model structure contributes to these variations in ROC analysis enrichment (Figure

6.3.2 3D visualisation of top scoring molecules
Three-dimensional visualization of the docking poses of the top three molecules provides
additional insights into the crucial binding interactions within the pocket that determine binding
affinity and activity. The generated molecules are required to exhibit structural similarities with
the reference compounds selected for the pharmacophore model, ensuring they encompass
bioactive functionality. Novel chemical structures may mitigate binding issues, side effects, or
complex synthesis pathways.
Figure 11A illustrates the binding interactions of crystallized CCR2-RA-[R]. Notably, there are
critical chemical features such as π-stacking with residue PHE312, where a halogen bond can
also be formed with VAL63 at the same benzene ring. Hydrogen bond interactions in the middle
of the molecule play an important stabilizing role with Glu310, Lys311 and PHE312.
Additionally, the side chains of the reference molecules occupy a lipophilic pocket around
residue VAL24422. Comp39 and CCX140 exhibit an additional hydrogen bond with residue
LYS237.
Figure 11B, C and D display the binding interactions of molecules 1, 2, and 3, further portraying
the key interactions governing their binding and activity.
Molecule 1 (Fig. 9 and 11B) exhibits close structural similarity to SD-24 (Fig. 10A), with both
molecules belonging to the sulphonamide class. The central sulphonamide group facilitates
hydrogen bonding within the core of the molecule. Additionally, the structure features a
benzene ring with a chlorine atom at the third position, enabling the formation of a halogen
bond with residue Val63 (highlighted in red) and π-stacking interactions with residues Tyr305
and Phe312 (highlighted in green). Furthermore, this benzene ring occupies the lipophilic
pocket between Val244 and Val308 (highlighted in yellow).
The most notable structural difference from the reference molecules is the presence of an
anisole. The oxygen atom of the anisole forms an additional hydrogen bond (highlighted in
blue) with residues Glu310 and Lys311 (highlighted in pink). This benzene ring also engages
in additional hydrophobic interactions with the surrounding residues. The binding interactions
of Molecule 1 closely overlap with the critical interactions observed in the reference molecules,
which explains its relatively high interaction score.
While Molecule 1 shows close similarity to SD-24, it differs by featuring an anisole instead of
the benzoic acid present in SD-24. Additionally, Molecule 1 lacks an extra halogen atom in the
17lipophilic pocket. It also shares no significant structural similarities with the other reference
molecules, reflected in its ROCS (similarity) score of 0.425.
Molecule 2 (Fig. 9 and 11C) is based on the imidazole pyridine scaffold of Comp39 (Fig. 10B),
with additional phenol and a methyl phenyl ether group that facilitate π-stacking interactions
with Tyr305 and Phe312. The imidazole pyridine scaffold forms hydrogen bonds with Glu310
and Lys311. Unlike Molecule 1, the imidazole pyridine scaffold in Molecule 2 features an
additional nitrogen atom that forms another hydrogen bond with Glu310. The methyl phenyl
ether group in Molecule 2 fits into the lipophilic pocket around Val244. The presence of
additional phenyl groups and the imidazole pyridine scaffold contribute to a higher ROCS
score of 0.625 compared to Molecule 1, as the reference molecules JNJ, CCX140, and Comp39
share similar structural groups. The lower interaction score of Molecule 2, relative to Molecule
1, can be attributed to the remaining space around Arg240 and Lys237, and the absence of a
halogen bond with Val63.
Molecule 3 shares structural similarity with Molecule 2, featuring a imidazole pyridine scaffold
in the centre and phenylethane and 1-(2-methylphenyl)ethane as side chains. In Fig. 11D, the
phenyl side chain engages in π-stacking interactions with Tyr305 and Phe312. The imidazole
pyridine scaffold forms hydrogen bonds with Glu310 and Lys311, while the methyl
phenylethane group occupies the lipophilic pocket near Val244. The ROCS score of Molecule
3 is similar to that of Molecule 2 due to their structural similarities (Fig 9). However, the
interaction score of Molecule 3 is influenced by the open space near Arg240 and the absence
of additional hydrogen bonds with Glu310 or Lys237 (highlighted in pink). These factors
contribute to its different binding interactions compared to Molecule 2.

7. Conclusion
We developed an in silico workflow that integrates shape-matching via pharmacophore
modelling with ROCS into the reinforcement loop of the de novo generation with DrugEx. This
approach yielded three novel molecules with high structural and binding similarity to known
intracellular allosteric binders of the chemokine receptor CCR2, while also exhibiting unique
structural differences. Initially applied to CCR2 antagonists, this workflow has potential for
broader application across various targets, particularly those with limited datasets for QSAR
modelling.
Our study demonstrated the promise of combining pharmacophore modelling with de novo
generation for discovering novel bioactive molecules. Future research should refine this
workflow and explore its application to diverse pharmacological targets. In particular, to
validate our findings, in vitro and in vivo assays are essential to determine binding affinities
and bioactivity. Furthermore, to determine if the top-scoring de novo molecules are already
catalogued, a search within the Enamine database54 is necessary. Should these structures not
yet exist, synthetic pathways can be identified using advanced tools such as AIZynthfinder55.
Additionally, molecular dynamics studies could offer deeper insights into significant binding
interactions. Further analysis of generated and docked structures, particularly those with
modifications, may reveal critical and novel binding interactions while also presenting more
diverse scaffolds.

Explanation
1. High-risk biological agent
High-risk pathogens refer in particular to pathogens of risk class 3 and 4 , which are the most serious
and deadly pathogens in the world. For example class 3: Yersinia pestis (black plague), HIV, SARS virus
and class 4: Ebola virus, Marburg virus, Lassa virus
2.Host range and tropism
Tropism is the ability of a biological agent, for example a virus, to infect certain target cells and tissues.
Changes in the tropism can result in the ability of the biological agent to infect other cells (for example
also lung cells instead of just liver cells) that were previously not susceptible. By adjusting the host
range, other types of hosts can be infected that were previously not susceptible to the biological
agent, for example from animal-to-animal (infectious animal disease) to animal-to-human (zoonosis).
On the other hand, a narrowing of the host range can potentially increase the efficiency of infection.
3.Virulence
Virulence refers to the amount of harm a biological agent may cause when infecting its host. This is
also called pathogenic capacity. The virulence of a bacterium is often determined by a virulence factor
which is a protein or a carbohydrate. The genetic code for such a virulence factor is often on a plasmid
that can be inherited along with the bacterial genome. For example, adding a virulence factor to a
non-pathogenic bacterium can make this bacterium more harmful.
4.Stability
Increasing stability indicates a change in the ability of a biological agent to remain infectious in the
environment when exposed to various environmental conditions. Increasing resistance to lower or
higher temperatures, dry or more humid environments, air pollution or sunlight are factors that can
play a role in this. Resistance to disinfectant also ensures greater stability of the biological agent or
toxin.
5.Transmissibility
Transmission of the biological agent can occur through inhalation, ingestion (oral), direct contact
(dermal), blood contact (puncture/cut accident) or via vectors. Transmission routes may be changed,
for example, from direct contact (touch) to air-borne transmission.
6.Absorption and toxicokinetics
Toxicokinetics refers to the kinetics and effect of a substance in the body. The kinetics are determined
by the absorption, distribution, metabolism, biotransformation and excretion of the substance by an
organism in relation to time. Toxicity is the relationship between exposure to a substance,
transformation and adverse effects. For example, a toxin may be modified to alter the toxicokinetics,
causing the toxin's harmful effect to last longer. Another example is the application of additional
proteins on the surface of a bacterium so that it is more easily absorbed by the body.
7.Drug resistance
The effectiveness of vaccines and therapeutic or prophylactic agents can be reduced if microorganisms
acquire different properties, for example through genetic modification. Adding antibiotic resistance to
26pathogenic bacteria can prevent successful antibiotic treatment. This applies not only to medical care,
but also, for example, to plant pathogens in the agricultural sector with (un)intended harmful effects
as a result. Another example is proteins that have been changed resulting in the inability of a drug to
bind, indicating that the drug becomes ineffective.
8.Population immunity
The immune system can be weakened by certain substances produced by viruses or bacteria, making
people, animals or plants more susceptible to (other) pathogens. Another example of a negative effect
on immunity is the expression of immune regulatory genes, preventing the initiation of a normal
immune response. In addition, by modifying a certain micro-organism to which a population has
developed (almost) complete immunity, this population might become susceptible again.
9.Detection methodology and diagnostics
The diagnosis (detection method) of a virus is often based on protein recognition or nucleic acid
recognition. By adapting specific proteins or the DNA or RNA sequence, the virus may no longer be
detected by conventional methods (for example immunoassay or PCR).
10. Reconstruction
Based on the knowledge of hereditary material, a new virus or bacterium can be synthesized or an
eradicated or extinct biological agent can be reconstructed. For example, smallpox has been
eradicated and people are no longer vaccinated against this virus. No population resistance exists
against the Spanish flu from 1918. Reconstructing such a pathogen is hazardous and laboratory
incidents can have major consequences.
11. Harmful effects
Your research may result in (un)intended outcomes, causing your biological agent to display properties
that have not been demonstrated before. This could potentially make the biological agent or toxin
interesting as a biological warfare agent (weaponization). This is possible, for example, when a
bacterium secrete a toxin in a higher concentration or if a different toxin is produced. In case of
indications of an increase in harmful effects, it may be necessary to raise the containment level
(biosafety level, BSL) or additional risk management measures may be required.
12. Knowledge and Technology
Knowledge and technologies, including equipment, techniques, bioinformatics and genomics, used by
or resulting from your research could potentially be misused. This does not directly involve the
harmful properties of the biological agent itself, but concerns the relevant knowledge, methodologies
and technologies used. Be aware that the results of your research can also be extrapolated to related
biological agents or applied in a different context. For example, a virus reconstruction method can also
be misused to synthesize a pandemic virus. Sharing knowledge does not only concern scientific
publications, but also deals with knowledge and technologies that are shared at, for example,
conferences, poster presentations, databases and via email even with foreign offices of your company
or partners in your research project. This implies that sharing or publishing may be subject to export
control measures.
13. Ecological consequences
Besides public health risks, other risks, such as consequences for the ecosystem should be considered
in case of misuse of the biological agent or knowledge thereof. For example, misuse of the application
of gene drives with the potential to genetically modify entire populations may have consequences for
entire ecosystems. Another example is the use of insects carrying genetically modified viruses to alter
crop chromosomes directly in the field through gene transfer. The idea is to protect crops against
pests and diseases, but this technology can also be used to destroy plants.
14. Economic consequences
Besides risks to humans and the environment, also other risks should be considered in case of misuse
27of a biological agent or the knowledge thereof. Consider, for example, the economic consequences of
a failed harvest or an export ban for products from livestock farming. If you work with a quarantine
plant pathogen or, for example, with the Foot and Mouth Disease virus, this can result in transport
bans and export restrictions with all economic consequences that entails in the event of (un) intended
spread.
15. Consequences for society
Misuse of a biological agent can also affect society and people's social well-being. Only the threat of a
biological agent, such as Bacillus anthracis, can cause social disturbance in civil society. This doesn’t
concern only high-risk biological agents. Applications with low and non-pathogenic biological agents
can also have negative social impact. Consider, for example, the production of addictive substances by
yeast. Microorganisms can also be used to restore the natural environment that has been altered by
pollutants to its original state. This remediation method is called bioremediation, but possibly can be
misused as well. An example is bacteria that can break down metals, whereby such biological agents
could also be used to destroy working electronics.
